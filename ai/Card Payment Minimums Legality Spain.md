Card Payment Minimums Legality Spain
====================================

**USER:**
is it legal for businesses in spain to put card payment minimum

**ASSISTANT:**
Yes — in Spain it's legal for businesses to set a minimum amount for card payments, though it's a somewhat surprising answer given how strict EU rules are elsewhere. Here's the picture based on the sources I found:

**What the law actually says**

Spain has no regulation that prohibits setting a minimum spend for card payments — and none that explicitly allows it either, which effectively leaves businesses free to do so ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/resources/more/minimum-amounts-for-card-payments-in-spain)). Under the latest Bank of Spain report, the average minimum applied is around €11, and practices of €5–10 minimums are common in small tapas bars and family-run shops ([Wise](https://wise.com/gb/blog/cash-or-card-in-spain)).

However, businesses doing this have obligations:

- **Clear signage** — the minimum (or the fact that cards aren't accepted) must be posted in a clearly visible location. If a shop refuses a card payment without having disclosed that, consumer group FACUA notes they should offer an alternative (e.g., a SEPA transfer or Bizum) rather than force cash on the customer.
- **No surcharges** — per Article 35.3 of Royal Decree-Law 19/2018, a business can never charge extra for paying by card, no matter how small the purchase. They may offer discounts for cash, though.
- **Cash must still be accepted** — since Royal Decree 24/2021 came into force in May 2022, retailers are generally obliged to accept cash, and refusing it can be a legal violation ([Cash Essentials](https://cashessentials.org/spain-new-law-requires-retailers-to-accept-cash)).
- **EU-level rule** — if a business offers card payment as an option at all, EU rules require them to accept card payments from customers anywhere in the EU, and surcharges on cards are banned across the EU ([Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/business/finance-and-tax/making-receiving-payments/electronic-cash-payments/index_en.htm)).

One caveat worth knowing: this is Spanish national practice — the card networks (Visa/Mastercard) themselves have historically discouraged or restricted minimums in their merchant agreements, so an individual merchant's contract with its payment processor could impose stricter terms than the law requires.

So in short: a €10 minimum sign at a Spanish café is legal, as long as it's clearly displayed, no card surcharge is added, and cash is still accepted.

so, in theory, a card payment minimum can be placed. But why do people say it is not true?

**ASSISTANT:**
Good question — the confusion is real, and it stems from a genuine legal grey zone. Here's where the disagreement comes from:

**1. There's no explicit rule either way — so both camps "have a point."**
As the Stripe analysis noted, no Spanish regulation prohibits minimums, but none permits them explicitly either. That vacuum means consumer advocates and merchants each fill the silence with their preferred interpretation. People who say it's illegal are essentially arguing from general principles rather than a specific ban.

**2. EU rules are commonly (mis)read as banning conditions on card acceptance.**
The EU's own guidance for merchants states that if you accept electronic payments, "you must accept the payment irrespective of where they or their payment service providers are located" ([Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/business/finance-and-tax/making-receiving-payments/electronic-cash-payments/index_en.htm)). Many people reasonably read this as "once you have a card machine, you must accept all card payments, full stop" — which would outlaw minimums. In practice this rule was written mainly to prevent discrimination against foreign/EU customers, not to regulate purchase-size conditions, but that nuance gets lost.

**3. Confusion with the surcharge ban.**
Article 35.3 of Royal Decree-Law 19/2018 firmly prohibits charging customers extra for paying by card. Since a €10 minimum "costs" small-basket customers something in inconvenience, people conflate "conditions that penalize card use" with "card surcharges" — but legally these are different things, and only the surcharge has an explicit ban.

**4. Consumer groups actively campaign against the practice.**
Organizations like FACUA take the position that if a business hasn't clearly disclosed a minimum, refusing the card payment is improper and the merchant must offer an alternative. Their public messaging — echoed widely on social media and forums (like the Reddit thread about the Asturian bar that surfaced in my earlier search) — simply asserts "it's illegal to set a minimum if you have a card machine." It gets repeated as fact, but it reflects an advocacy interpretation, not a settled court ruling or regulatory decision that I could verify.

**5. Card network contracts may be stricter than national law.**
Visa and Mastercard rules historically restricted minimums in many markets, and a merchant violating their processor agreement faces a different kind of consequence. Someone told "you can't do this" by their payment provider may assume it's law.

So the honest bottom line: the claim "minimums are illegal in Spain" is an unverified assertion repeated confidently. As far as documented law goes, the practice sits in an ungoverned middle ground — legal by default, but constrained by disclosure requirements, the surcharge ban, cash-acceptance obligations, and possible card-scheme contract rules. If someone has a concrete source claiming an explicit prohibition (e.g., a specific court ruling or consumer-authority fine), I'd genuinely be interested to see it, since my searches didn't surface one.
